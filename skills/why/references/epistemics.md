# Epistemics

How to rate confidence when evidence is historical, partial and sometimes contradictory, and how to say it without false certainty.

A thing does not carry its own motive. You can see a charge, a rule or a setting. Its reason lives in mail, chats, notes, terms and announcements, all incomplete and sometimes missing.

## Claims about human decisions

When the scope of a person's ruling matters, find their original words. Keep a source pointer, the speaker, and enough of the wording to fix the scope. Label an agent summary as a summary, whatever its age. If the original is not available, say so and leave the scope uncertain. Never invent a firm restriction or permission.

The owner's "don't book Indigo this month" and a note that says "the owner never flies Indigo" are different claims. Report the scoped original and the broader reading apart. Copies of one reading in several notes are one evidence chain, not independent decisions: trace them to their source before you raise confidence. Ordinary facts do not need verbatim quotes.

## Confidence tiers

Every claim sits in one tier. The tier sets its section and its wording.

1. **Direct.** A person or organization wrote why. "Your flight was cancelled due to a crew shortage." Say it with confidence, in the present tense, with the citation: "This exists because X."
2. **Supported.** Several indirect pieces converge, but none states it. The charge date matches a renewal in the order history, and the amount matches the plan price. "The evidence points strongly to X: [the pieces]." Cite each piece.
3. **Inferred.** A reasonable reading with no explicit support. Hedge it ("likely", "appears", "is consistent with"), and show the chain: "Given A and B, C seems likely because D."
4. **Speculative.** Plausible, but thin, and other explanations fit as well. "One possibility is X, but we have no direct evidence." It goes under competing explanations.
5. **Unknown.** You looked and could not find out. Say exactly what you searched: "We searched mail for the merchant name and amount since January, read the card's fee schedule, and searched past chats. None explained the charge."

## Wording

- "because", "the reason is", "was designed to", "fixes", "decided" claim Direct or Supported confidence. Put a citation next to each.
- Hedge words ("appears", "likely", "suggests", "is consistent with", "plausibly", "may have been") mark an inference.
- Cut "obviously", "clearly", "of course", "just" and "I think". Write "the evidence suggests".

## Traps

- **Rationalization.** What makes sense today may rest on reasons that no longer apply or were wrong. Do not assume the other party did the right thing and work backward. Do not assume a pattern was intended. An absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
- **The owner's hypothesis.** "Is it the hotel's mistake?" is one candidate. Test it against the others. Confirm it only with citations.
- **Contradiction.** When two sources disagree, show both with citations. Do not pick the tidier story.
- **Missing evidence.** An honest "we don't know" is a valid answer: the owner learns where it is not, and who to ask (support, the sender, the other party). Offer to draft that message. Name each gap: the question, the sources, the searches, what you found.

## Check before you finish

1. Every claim in "What we found" and "What we can infer" has a citation, or moves down a tier.
2. The wording matches the tier.
3. No thing is cited as evidence of its own motive.
4. "What we don't know" exists and names specific gaps. An empty one is suspicious.
